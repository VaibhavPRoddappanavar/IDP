Reviewer: 2

Comments:
The manuscript proposes a DQN-lite based adaptive inference system to manage thermal throttling for YOLOv8 pothole detection on edge devices. The systematic progression from baseline profiling to a reinforcement learning controller is well-structured and addresses a genuine hardware constraint in embedded vision systems. However, the study suffers from critical flaws regarding its dangerous frame-skipping strategy in autonomous driving, ignorance of edge-cloud offloading paradigms, lack of stochastic latency modeling, and reliance on desktop hardware emulation rather than physical edge devices. Addressing the following major comments is necessary before the manuscript can be considered for publication.
In Section IV.B, the system's emergency fallback (Mode 2) relies on dropping the resolution to 320x320 and skipping intermediate frames to reduce the thermal load. For an autonomous vehicle or dashboard camera traveling at highway speeds, skipping frames creates massive blind spots in perception, which is a severe safety hazard. Instead of severely degrading local detection fidelity or blindly skipping frames, modern intelligent transportation systems employ dynamic offloading mechanisms. As established in recent literature such as "Deterministic Task Offloading and Resource Allocation in the IoT-Edge-Cloud Continuum", leveraging the edge-cloud architecture allows resource-constrained edge nodes to dynamically offload heavy computational tasks to edge servers when local thermal or processing limits are reached. The authors must discuss why a deterministic task offloading paradigm was not considered as a safe fallback mechanism instead of dangerous frame skipping, and how their system could integrate with the IoT-Edge-Cloud continuum to maintain continuous high-fidelity detection under thermal stress.
The manuscript repeatedly claims to offer a "Real-Time" inference system and uses deterministic FPS and Latency metrics (Equation 1). However, in practical autonomous systems and IoT environments, video streams generated by sensors or transmitted over vehicular networks do not arrive with perfectly deterministic intervals. They are heavily subjected to arrival-time jitter and traffic variability. As mathematically demonstrated in the study "Stochastic End-to-End Latency Modeling of the IoT-Edge-Cloud Continuum: Impact of Jitter and Traffic Variability on Deterministic Service Provisioning", ignoring stochastic temporal variabilities inevitably leads to massive queueing delays, rendering static latency calculations invalid. If the system dynamically changes processing speeds or offloads tasks, the queueing delay will fluctuate severely due to jitter. The authors must revise their deterministic latency claims and incorporate a discussion on how their DQN-lite state space and reward function account for stochastic frame arrival jitter and dynamic queueing delays in real-world deployments.
The experimental setup in Section V.A states that testing was conducted on a computer system "emulating the profile of an NVIDIA Jetson". Thermal dynamics, ambient temperature absorption (e.g., a dashboard under direct sunlight), and physical heatsink saturation cannot be accurately emulated on a desktop PC. The authors must validate their reinforcement learning controller on physical edge hardware (e.g., an actual Jetson Orin Nano or Xavier NX) to prove the system's thermal management actually works in practice.
The performance evaluation focuses heavily on the overall Mean Average Precision (mAP) drop. However, potholes are predominantly small-scale objects in the camera's field of view. Downscaling the input resolution from 640x640 to 320x320 (Mode M2) disproportionately destroys the network's ability to detect small objects. The authors must report the specific mAP for small objects across the different inference modes to reveal the true safety cost of thermal throttling on pothole detection.
In Section IV.E, the Deep Q-Network (DQN-lite) is introduced. Reinforcement learning agents inherently require an exploration phase to learn optimal policies. If the agent explores randomly during a real-world driving scenario, it could intentionally trigger thermal overload or drop frames inappropriately. The authors must explain how the exploration-exploitation trade-off is managed safely during online adaptation without jeopardizing vehicle safety.
The state space defined in Equation 2 includes hardware metrics like GPU utilization, CPU load, and temperature. However, it completely ignores the vehicle's speed. Dropping frames while a vehicle is stopped at a traffic light is perfectly safe, but dropping frames while traveling at 100 kilometers per hour is highly dangerous. The authors should justify why vehicle speed or visual frame context was excluded from the state vector.
The system architecture (Figure 1) relies heavily on Python, ctypes, and OpenCV running in background threads. Python's Global Interpreter Lock (GIL) is known to cause stuttering and unpredictable latency spikes in multi-threaded real-time computer vision pipelines. The authors must provide a rigorous latency profiling of the Python overhead, or explain why a lower-level implementation (e.g., C++ with TensorRT native threads) was not utilized to guarantee strict real-time execution.
The reward function (Equation 3) relies on three hyperparameters: alpha, beta, and delta. The manuscript provides no details on how these weights were selected. A sensitivity analysis is required to demonstrate how varying these arbitrary weights impacts the agent's preference for maintaining frame rates versus preventing thermal spikes.
In Section IV.F, the authors mention using TensorRT FP16 optimizations. TensorRT natively supports "Dynamic Shapes," allowing the inference engine to accept variable input resolutions efficiently without reloading the execution context. It is unclear if the proposed system utilizes TensorRT's native dynamic resolution capabilities or if it relies on a custom, less efficient resizing wrapper. This implementation detail must be clarified.
The comparative analysis in Section V compares the learning-based controller only against a static baseline and a simple rule-based heuristic. To prove the superiority of the DQN-lite approach, the authors should include a comparison against existing dynamic inference frameworks or built-in hardware thermal governors (like Nvidia's nvpmodel power profiles) that manage thermals at the operating system level.
